Independent research project · Summer 2026

By Coralyn Wu · UCLA

Can a fully determined universe fake quantum weirdness?

I built small mathematical “universes” in code and tested whether quantum-style correlations could grow out of them on their own, without breaking the laws of physics and without cheating. Along the way I measured the exact price such a universe has to pay.

01 · The puzzle

A 90-year-old argument about whether the universe plays dice

A GAME NO PLAN CAN WIN

Two players, far apart

Alice and Bob sit in different cities. Each is asked one of two random questions and must answer yes or no, with no way to talk. Their answers get a . In 1964 John Bell proved that no pre-arranged plan can score above 2. Pairs of entangled particles, tested in real labs, score about 2.83.

THREE WAYS OUT

Something we assumed is wrong

Bell’s proof rests on three assumptions. At least one has to go:

localityinfluences can travel faster than light
realismoutcomes don’t exist until measured
free choicethe questions were never truly independent
THE PATH I TOOK

Superdeterminism

If everything, including which question gets asked, follows from the universe’s starting conditions, the particles don’t need spooky signals: they were always correlated with the questions. Bell himself noticed this loophole in 1985 but found it too strange to pursue. It is the only option that keeps the universe both deterministic and local.

The catch is a price. To fake the quantum pattern, the hidden state of the particles has to “know” a little about the questions. Information theory measures that knowledge in . In 2011 the physicist Michael Hall proved the minimum is 0.0663 bit, less than a tenth of a single yes/no answer.

My question: can I build an actual rule, a tiny universe in code, that pays close to that price, follows the rules of physics, and produces the quantum curve by itself rather than because I programmed it in?

Why this is hard

The most concrete version of superdeterminism (Tim Palmer’s invariant-set theory) says the universe lives on a : an infinitely jagged shape. But the tools that can learn rules from data, like , only work on smooth shapes. Getting “fractal” and “learnable” into the same model was the central problem of the project.

02 · What I built

Four tools, each one checked on a problem with a known answer first

MEASURE THE PRICE

An exact calculator that, given any target pattern, finds the minimum information a universe must “leak” to produce it.

convex optimization · cvxpy
LEARN RULES

Neural networks that work backwards from a target pattern to the rule that could produce it, instead of just fitting data points.

physics-informed neural networks · PyTorch
BUILD FRACTAL WORLDS

Small dynamical systems that squeeze space into fractal shapes, measured with a fractal-dimension estimator.

chaos theory · dissipative maps · NumPy
FIND READABLE FORMULAS

A genetic algorithm I wrote that evolves actual equations, so the result is a formula a person can read, not a black box.

· self-built engine
03 · Results

What came out of it

Result 1 · Verification

I independently reproduced a published result

Before trusting my tools on new questions, I tested them on an old one. Starting from scratch, my calculator recovered Hall’s minimum price for the quantum curve: 0.0667 bit against his 0.0663 bit, a difference of about 0.3%, with the peak at the same angle.

The curve shows the minimum “peeking” needed at each detector angle. It is zero at 0°, 90° and 180°, where classical physics already matches nature, and peaks in between.

Result 2 · A new pattern

The price follows a simple law

I asked how the price grows as the score rises from the classical limit (2) toward the quantum limit (2.83). The answer is close to a square: price ≈ 0.0665 × (score − 2)², fitting the data with R² = 0.9993.

Beating classical physics by a little is nearly free, and each extra step costs more. The curve also passes straight through the quantum limit without any bend, which tells us this price alone can’t explain why nature stops at 2.83.

This is a numerical fit, not a proof. It also held up when I extended the setup to more questions, more possible answers, and more particles.

Result 3 · The hardest property

The quantum curve grew out of a fractal world

My first fractal system (the Hénon map) blew up numerically during training. I replaced it with a bounded “baker” map that folds space like dough and can never escape its box. In three dimensions, the quantum curve emerged on a genuine fractal (dimension 1.55, well below the 3 of solid space), matching the target closely.

Honest caveat: this worked for 3 of 5 random restarts, not all of them, and the fractal came from a hand-built map with no simple formula. Result 4 fixes the second problem.

Result 4 · Interpretability

A formula a person can read

Neural networks give answers you can’t read. So I wrote a genetic algorithm that evolves equations directly. It found the rule f(x) = cos(x) · sin(x), and five independent searches all passed the accuracy threshold.

The key idea that finally let “fractal” and “readable” coexist: let the structure of the system (steady squeezing in some directions) create the fractal, and let smooth, readable formulas steer it.

Getting here took three versions of the scoring rule. Each earlier version was fooled by a solution that looked right but wasn’t, which is exactly what the rigor section below is about.

Result 5 · End to end

Every part of the model was discovered, not designed

In the final version, the search found the rule of the universe, the way its state is read out, and the directions the two detectors point, all at once. Every one of five searches passed every check, each with a different formula. The only things I set by hand were the goal and the anti-cheating checks.

What this does and doesn’t show: the effective score (≈ 2.80) is converted from the curve’s shape, not measured in a separate Bell test, and the information price matches Hall’s bound largely because the curve matches. See “Open questions”.

04 · Rigor

How I kept myself from fooling myself

In a project like this, the easiest result to get is a wrong one that looks right. I followed four rules the whole way through.

RULE 1

Calibrate first

Every new tool had to get a known answer right before I used it on an unknown one.

RULE 2

Repeat with new seeds

Anything random was rerun with several starting points, so one lucky run couldn’t decide a result.

RULE 3

Too good means check

A result that beat expectations triggered a code review, not a celebration.

RULE 4

Failures count

Dead ends are recorded as results. Unverified numbers were withdrawn rather than reported.

Four of the 11 errors I caught

  • FAKEA search scored 3.40, above the quantum maximum of 2.83. Impossible for real physics, so I checked: the model was secretly letting one side see the other side’s question. Discarded.
  • FAKEA formula scored 3.11 even though its curve was nearly perfect. The scoring formula was amplifying a tiny error at one angle threefold. I replaced it with a fit over the whole curve.
  • DEGENERATEOne solution only looked good because “Alice” gave the same answer 99.8% of the time. I added a check that both sides must genuinely answer both ways.
  • BUGNumbers silently collapsed to zero after about 52 steps because the computer ran out of decimal precision. I changed the formula and added an automatic guard.

At the end I audited all 120 code files (18,040 lines) specifically for bugs, cheating patterns and claims the code didn’t support. It found no fabricated data, but several places where my wording was stronger than the evidence. I rewrote those.

05 · Open questions

What this project does not show yet

  • The true price of my model was never measured directly. I computed the minimum price any model would pay for the same curve. Measuring what my specific model actually pays needs tools that don’t work well in high dimensions yet.
  • The score of ≈ 2.8 is derived, not tested. It comes from the shape of the curve, not from a separate four-question Bell experiment on the model.
  • The fractal is sometimes thin. In a few runs the fractal was close to a simple line, and the match to the quantum curve is close but not exact.

These are the next steps, not bugs. They are listed in full in the technical archive.
